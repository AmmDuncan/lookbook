Lookbook · Chapter 13

Site archetypes.

Marketing covered hero / features / pricing / CTA — the parts every site has. This chapter covers the parts every site needs but most treat as afterthoughts: about, careers, case study, blog, docs, changelog, status, legal, 404. Each has a shape. Get the shape right; the system you've built does the rest.

01 — About

About page #about

The story, plainly.

About is the most-misused page on most sites. It's not a brand essay — it's the page where a prospect decides if you're real. Show the people, the founding moment, and the metrics. Skip the mission-vision-values.

  • 01
    Hero — one-sentence company story8–14 words. What you do, for whom.
  • 02
    Origin — why this existsOne photo, two paragraphs.
  • 03
    Three numbers that earn trustUsers · customers · funding · longevity.
  • 04
    The team gridReal portraits. Names + roles. Link to LinkedIn.
  • 05
    What we believe — 3 principles, maxSkip values lists. State positions.
  • 06
    CTA — usually careers or contactNot "sign up." Wrong moment.
Real founder portraits beat stock office shots, always.

Roles list, with a reason to apply.

Two failure modes: (1) a 2000-word company manifesto, (2) just a Greenhouse iframe. The right answer is a short pitch, three benefits, then the role list — sortable by team and location.

  • 01
    Hero — what's it like to work hereOne sentence. One real photo.
  • 02
    Three benefits that aren't "free snacks"Equity, autonomy, learning budget, remote.
  • 03
    Open roles, by teamFilter: team · location. Show count.
  • 04
    "No fit right now" CTAGeneral-interest form for great people.
  • 05
    Recruiter-blocked footerRecruiters please don't contact us.
Every role title is a verb people would say out loud — never "Strategic Operations Lead II."
03 — Case study

Customer case study #case

Numbers in the hero. Story underneath.

The case study lives or dies on the first screen. Three numbers, one quote, one face. Then the longer story below — for the reader who's already convinced and wants the details.

  • 01
    Customer name + tagline"Acme reduced support tickets 47% with…"
  • 02
    Hero photo — the product or the userNever a generic office shot.
  • 03
    Three stats, big and boldIncrease X · saved Y · faster than Z.
  • 04
    Quote with face and roleReal person. Real role. Linkable.
  • 05
    Problem → solution → resultThree sections, equal weight.
  • 06
    Related case studies + CTA"Talk to a customer like you."
One template, used six times. Don't bespoke each case study.
04 — Blog index

Blog / journal index #blog-index

One image ratio. One card. One rhythm.

Most blog indexes fail by trying to be a magazine — featured hero card, sidebar, multiple layouts. Pick one card, repeat it, filter by tag. Discoverability beats variety.

  • 01
    Title + tag filters5–8 tags max. Active state is the accent.
  • 02
    Card grid — 3 cols desktop, 1 mobile16:9 image · date · title · 2-line excerpt.
  • 03
    Pagination or "load more"Whichever matches the post volume.
  • 04
    RSS link in the footerThe actual readers want it.
Don't bespoke "featured" cards — the system has one card, used N times.

Reading first. Decoration last.

Long-form is one place a serif belongs. The whole page is built for one job: keep the reader on the page. Hero photo optional. Author + date essential. Pull-quotes, figures, and code blocks all share a single rhythm.

  • 01
    Eyebrow — category + read timeSets expectation in 6 words.
  • 02
    Title — display weight, generous trackingOptional serif for editorial register.
  • 03
    Author + date + shareSmall. Right under the title.
  • 04
    Body at 720px max-widthMeasure is ~65 characters per line.
  • 05
    Figures, pull-quotes, code blocksEach breaks the rhythm intentionally.
  • 06
    Related posts + newsletter signupThe page ends with a next click.
Body type at 18px. Line-height 1.55–1.65. Anything tighter is hostile.
06 — Docs home

Docs landing #docs-home

⌘K search

Search above the fold. Quick-starts below.

Docs home is a wayfinding page, not a content page. Three things matter, in order: search, the top-level categories, the most-clicked starting points. Spend the budget on the article template — that's where users actually land.

  • 01
    Title — what the docs cover"API · SDK · Guides for X."
  • 02
    Search bar (⌘K)Hero element. Algolia / Typesense / built-in.
  • 03
    3–4 top-level category cardsGuides · Reference · Tutorials · API.
  • 04
    Popular starting pointsTop 6 articles by traffic. Save the new user a click.
  • 05
    Community + changelog + status footerThree links. Not a full nav.
If your docs home is longer than this, it's a marketing page in disguise.

Dated entries. Conventional tags. RSS.

The most under-designed page on every site. Three things make it usable: a real date on every entry, tags for change type, and an RSS feed for the users who care. Don't gamify it.

  • 01
    Title + RSS + SubscribeThree icons in the corner. That's the header.
  • 02
    Reverse-chronological entriesNewest first. Permalink per entry.
  • 03
    Tags: Added · Improved · Fixed · RemovedFour colors. Held consistent across releases.
  • 04
    Body — short paragraphs, no buried ledeUser-facing changes only. Internal refactors are noise.
  • 05
    Inline image / video for visual changesA 30s loop beats 200 words.
Every entry is permalinkable. People share these.
09 — Status

Status page #status

Honest, immediate, scannable.

Users land here panicked. The job is to confirm "yes, we know" in under a second. Big overall-status pill at the top; service-by-service breakdown below; the most recent incident's note in plain language.

  • 01
    One overall-status pillOperational · Degraded · Down. Dot + word.
  • 02
    Service rows with 90-day historyBar chart of uptime per service.
  • 03
    Active incident with timelineInvestigating → Identified → Monitoring → Resolved.
  • 04
    Subscribe by email / SMS / webhookRight side. Persistent.
  • 05
    Past incidents — last 30 daysHonesty over polish.
A green page during a real outage is worse than a yellow page during a small one. Update fast.

Terms of Service

Readable. Versioned. Tracked.

A legal page is still a page. Single column at reading width, numbered sections, a real TOC, a clear last-updated stamp. Effort here signals trust the same way a great about page does.

  • 01
    Title + version + last-updated stampReal semantic version, not "updated recently."
  • 02
    Numbered TOC, sticky on desktopPeople deep-link to specific clauses.
  • 03
    Body at reading widthSame body style as long-form blog.
  • 04
    "Plain-language summary" box per sectionOptional but loved. Not legal advice.
  • 05
    Changelog at the bottom — what changed and whenBuilds trust the same as any other changelog.
Same body styles as the blog. The legal page is not a different brand.
11 — 404

The not-found page #404

404

One sentence. Two actions. Don't try to be clever.

The 404 is where most sites attempt humor and fail. The actual job: tell the user what happened, give them somewhere to go, and look like the rest of the site. The signature moment, if you want one, belongs on the homepage — not here.

  • 01
    Code — 404 in your display typeBig. Brand-consistent. No mascots.
  • 02
    Plain-language explanation"The page you wanted has moved or doesn't exist."
  • 03
    Primary action — go homeOr "back to where you were."
  • 04
    Secondary — search or contactFor the user who's truly stuck.
If your 404 takes longer than your homepage to design, you're avoiding harder problems.

The supporting pages are the brand test.

Anyone can design a beautiful homepage. The career-level question is whether the changelog, the 404, the legal page, and the careers list all feel like the same site. When they do, you've built a system — not a campaign.
